Negative Days on a Business Bank Statement: What a Funder Reads Into Them

A day that ends below zero tells a funder more than the revenue line. Most of those days come from timing, and timing can be moved.

By Edward M. Liceaga · December 9, 2026

Negative days on a business bank statement are the days your account closed below zero. A funder reading three months of statements counts them early, usually before the revenue total gets a second look.

I co-founded Amsterdam Capital Group, a direct funder. This is how that count gets read, and what you can do about it before you apply.

What counts as a negative day

The number that matters is the ending balance for the day. Your statement lists it, and a reviewer goes down that column looking for anything under zero.

Two related items sit next to those days. An overdraft means the bank paid something you did not have the money for, and usually charged a fee. A returned item, often marked NSF, means the bank refused the payment. A returned item reads worse, because somebody already tried to collect from the account and could not.

How the count gets read

One or two negative days in 90 is noise. Six or more starts to say the account cannot absorb what already comes out of it, and it says so more loudly when the days cluster at the same point every month.

An advance adds a daily or weekly payment to the account. If the account already ends some days below zero, the new payment will land on those days too. I walked through that math in merchant cash advance daily payment.

So the count changes the offer. It is the item that most often turns a strong looking file into a smaller amount or a no. I listed it third of five in what underwriters look for in bank statements, and it is the easiest of the five to fix.

Most negative days come from timing

Take an example with round numbers. It is an illustration, not a client file and not an offer, and it leaves out every bill except two.

A shop deposits $50,000 a month in card sales, about $2,300 each business day. Rent is $6,000 and one payroll run is $9,000. Both clear on the 1st. The account starts the month holding $7,000.

On the 1st, $2,300 comes in and $15,000 goes out. The day ends at negative $5,700. The 2nd ends at negative $3,400 and the 3rd at negative $1,100. The account is back above zero on the 4th.

That is three negative days a month and nine across a 90 day file. The shop took in $50,000 every one of those months.

Now suppose the landlord agrees to take rent on the 8th. On the 1st, $2,300 comes in and $9,000 goes out, and the day ends at $300. By the 8th about five more days of sales have arrived, so the rent clears with room to spare.

Nine negative days became none, and the shop earned exactly what it earned before. A real account has more bills than two, so run this on your own statements before you trust it.

Four ways to stop them

Move a debit you control. Rent, insurance, a supplier autopay, and a card payment all have a date somebody picked. Call and ask for a different one. Look first at whatever clears within a day or two of payroll.

Keep a floor. Choose a balance the operating account does not go under and treat that number as zero.

Stop sweeping to zero. If you move cash to savings the day it lands, the transfer back tends to arrive a day late. Leave a working balance where the bills clear.

Deposit everything through the business account. Cash that never reaches the account cannot cover a debit, and it does not count as revenue in the file either.

If the days are already on your statements

Three months is the standard file, so 90 days of clean statements replaces the old ones. If you can wait, waiting is the cheapest fix there is.

If you cannot wait, explain the days in the application. Say what caused them and what you changed. A cluster from one bad week that the owner names up front reads better than the same cluster nobody mentions. The reviewer will see it either way.

When more capital is the wrong answer

Timing causes most negative days, and some come from margin. If the account goes negative because less comes in than goes out, moving a rent date will not help, and neither will an advance. A new payment on top of a shortfall makes the shortfall bigger.

Work out which kind you have before you apply anywhere. Add up three months of deposits and three months of withdrawals. If deposits are comfortably higher and you still see negative days, you have a calendar problem, and you can fix that yourself.
