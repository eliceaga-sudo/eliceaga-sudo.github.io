When to Place a Defaulted MCA Account: The Cost of Waiting

The merchant says next week. The file gets harder to collect every week the promise gets repeated.

By Edward M. Liceaga · November 14, 2026

Every funder has this file. A missed payment, then a phone call and a promise. A partial ACH that clears, then another promise. Nobody wants to place an account that might still cure, so the file sits in internal servicing another week, and then another.

I'm a shareholder in Lexington Recovery and run its business development. We work defaulted merchant cash advance and other commercial receivables files for funders and lenders. The question I hear most often is when to place a defaulted account, and the honest answer is earlier than most funders do.

The short answer

Lexington Recovery's own guidance is to place the account when the merchant defaults under the contract. That can sound aggressive until you separate placement from litigation. Placement starts with a review of the documents, a strategy for the file, and outreach aimed at negotiation. Escalation comes later, and only when the file and the balance justify it.

What erodes while a funder waits

First, the money moves. A merchant who has decided not to pay usually changes how revenue arrives before anything else: a new bank account, a new processor, settlements pointed somewhere new. The deposits a funder could have reached in the first weeks are often gone by the time the file is placed.

The stack grows too. A merchant under pressure takes more advances, and every later position is another party with a claim on the same revenue. A settlement that ignores the other creditors tends to default again. In one of Lexington Recovery's published case studies, a restaurant file of about $33,000 was resolved with a plan built around the merchant's other positions from the start.

The guarantor's picture changes as well. Personal accounts move and assets change hands, so the secondary repayment source that looked solid at funding can look very different months later.

Silence turns into a habit. Every missed call that produces no consequence teaches the merchant that the funder will wait, and the longer that lesson runs, the harder the first real conversation gets.

And the record goes stale. Statements, call notes, and the timeline of promises are most useful while they are current, and easiest to assemble while the servicing team still remembers them.

Why funders wait anyway

The reasons are real, and each one deserves a straight answer.

"The merchant might renew." A merchant who isn't paying the current position isn't a renewal candidate. Protecting the relationship means getting the file back to performing, and sometimes that takes a different voice than the one the merchant has stopped answering.

"A partial payment came in." A partial payment tells you something, but it doesn't cure the default. Sometimes it signals a merchant working back toward good standing. Sometimes it buys time while the account gets moved. The file usually shows which.

"Placement costs money." Lexington Recovery structures engagements on a performance basis, and it's worth being precise about what that means: if nothing is recovered, the client still owes any applicable court fees and approved special services. Funders should hear that before they place a file.

"We don't want to be associated with aggressive collections." This is the most legitimate concern of all, and it's why the choice of recovery partner matters as much as the timing. A negotiation first approach protects the funder's name, while a collector that treats merchants as targets costs more reputation than it recovers.

"Our team can handle it." Internal servicing is good at reminders and early workouts. It usually isn't built for tracing undisclosed processors, finding out who actually pays the merchant, or coordinating enforcement across states.

Have these ready before placement

Lexington Recovery asks for three documents at the start of an engagement: the fully executed contract, the time stamped UCC-1, and the merchant's bank statements or receivables records. Add the servicing notes, any other positions you know about, and the guarantor's details, and the review can start the same day the file comes in.

Set the trigger before the file defaults

The funders who handle this well decide their placement rule in advance: contractual default plus a set number of days without a credible plan, written down and applied to every file the same way. It takes the emotion out of the decision and keeps the most persuasive merchants from getting the most time.
