Receivable Lien Against a Payment Processor: When It Works and When It Backfires

The most effective move in MCA recovery is also the most misused. The difference is whether the paperwork holds.

By Edward M. Liceaga · November 14, 2026

A merchant stops paying and tells the funder the business is struggling. The bank statements agree. Deposits are down to a trickle, and the settlement offer on the table is a steep discount.

Most recovery conversations start and end with the number the merchant shows you. The better question is where the revenue actually went, because a business that is still open is still selling something.

I'm a shareholder in Lexington Recovery and run its business development. We work defaulted merchant cash advance and other commercial receivables files for funders and lenders. The payment processor question comes up constantly, and it's where the best recoveries and the worst mistakes in this industry sit right next to each other.

Where card revenue goes when a file goes bad

Card revenue settles through a processor before it lands in a bank account. When a merchant defaults, the settlement path is one of the first things that changes. A new merchant account gets opened with a different processor. Settlements get pointed at a bank account the funder has never seen. The deposits on the statements shrink, and the sales that produced them do not.

The signals are usually visible to anyone looking for them. Card deposits drop to zero while the business is plainly still operating. An unfamiliar settlement name appears in the bank descriptors. The merchant's story about slow sales does not match the foot traffic, the reviews, or the posted hours.

What finding it looks like in practice

One of Lexington Recovery's published case studies is a retail ecommerce file with a balance of about $112,000. The merchant stopped paying, claimed hardship, and pushed for a steep discount, with financial disclosures that did not add up.

During negotiation, the account manager reviewed screenshots the merchant had provided and noticed a call with a payment processor that had never been disclosed. Tracing the number confirmed an active merchant account. A processor receivable hold and a bank hold together secured about $30,000, and the remaining balance moved into a weekly repayment plan.

That's one file, and it isn't a forecast. Results vary with the paperwork, the priority position, and the merchant. What the case does show is the method. The leverage came from reading the evidence the merchant had already sent.

What a notice to the processor actually does

Under Section 9-406 of the Uniform Commercial Code, once an account debtor receives proper notice that a right to payment has been assigned, it generally has to pay the assignee rather than the original party. In a merchant cash advance, the funder has purchased a share of the merchant's future receivables, and the processor is the party paying those receivables out.

When the notice is valid, a receivable lien against a payment processor is the most direct path in recovery, because it reaches revenue before that revenue ever touches an account the merchant controls.

Why it so often backfires

The notice only binds when the underlying position holds up. The assignment has to be valid and enforceable, and the funder's claim has to be senior to anyone else with rights in the same receivables. An earlier lender with a perfected security interest will usually come first.

The processor is also entitled to ask for reasonable proof of the assignment before it redirects anything. Commentary in ABF Journal made the point bluntly: these notices are widely used in the MCA market, their enforceability depends entirely on a valid and senior assignment, and an account debtor that doesn't get adequate proof should keep paying the merchant in the ordinary course.

A notice that does not hold is worse than no notice. The processor relationship freezes, the merchant gets a legitimate grievance and often a lawyer, and the funder's name ends up attached to exactly the kind of collection move that draws scrutiny to the whole industry. The file is less collectible than it was the day before.

The sequence that holds up

Paper first. A fully executed agreement, a time stamped UCC-1, and receivables language that says what the funder thinks it says. If any of those is missing, the processor conversation is premature.

Priority second. Who filed first, and what other positions exist. A merchant carrying several advances rarely has one clean senior funder, and the strategy needs to reflect that before anyone sends a demand.

Counsel before notice. Whether a specific notice binds a specific processor is a legal judgment that turns on the agreement, the filing, and the state. Lexington Recovery coordinates with counsel on that question rather than guessing at it.

Proof attached. A notice that arrives with the assignment and the priority evidence already enclosed answers the processor's reasonable proof question before it gets asked.

Keep talking. The point of the leverage is to reopen a negotiation that had stalled. In the case above, the holds came alongside a weekly repayment plan for the balance.

The recovery gets decided at funding

Most of what makes this work happens long before default. Funders who capture processor details at underwriting, file the UCC-1 promptly, and keep their receivables language precise are the ones whose files can support a receivable lien against a payment processor later.
