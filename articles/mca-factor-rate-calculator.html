How to read the results

The total payback and cost of capital are fixed the moment the file funds. They do not accrue and they do not discount if you finish early. If a funder offers an early payoff discount, that is a specific term you get in writing, not something to assume.

The annualized figure is there for one purpose: comparing against a bank APR honestly. A 1.3 factor over six months is not 30% interest, and anyone comparing it to an 11% bank quote should be comparing the annualized number instead. It is an approximation based on an evenly amortizing balance. Real remittance schedules vary, which is one more reason to treat everything on this page as arithmetic, not an offer.

The percentage line is the test that decides it. In my experience, a remittance under roughly 8% of weekly deposits is something most operations absorb. Between 8% and 15% you need a real answer for what the capital produces. Above 15%, the advance is likely to cause the problem it was supposed to solve, and no factor rate makes that different. Those bands are my rule of thumb from working files, not underwriting criteria.

The question the calculator cannot answer

What the money does. A cost of capital is only expensive or cheap relative to what the capital produces and what waiting costs. $15,000 of cost against $30,000 of margin on a discounted inventory buy works. $15,000 against a gap with no plan behind it does not, at any factor rate.

If the percentage line comes back comfortable and you have a real answer for what the money produces, the rest is paperwork.
